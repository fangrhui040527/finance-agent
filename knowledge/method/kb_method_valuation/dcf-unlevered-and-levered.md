1. Two consistent pairs
Free cash flow to the firm is operating profit after tax, plus depreciation, minus capital expenditure, minus the increase in working capital. It belongs to all capital providers and is discounted at the weighted average cost of capital; the result is enterprise value, and equity value is enterprise value minus net debt. Free cash flow to equity subtracts interest after tax and net debt repayment and is discounted at the cost of equity; the result is equity value directly. Mixing the pairs, unlevered cash flows at the cost of equity or levered cash flows at the WACC, is one of the most frequent errors found in published analyst models.

2. The drivers, each with a reason
Revenue growth, operating margin, reinvestment (capital expenditure and working capital as a share of revenue) and the tax rate drive the explicit forecast. A five-year projection with flat percentages is not a forecast; each driver needs a reason tied to history or to a stated change. This system's default scenarios take growth from the five-year compound rate capped at the terminal ceiling, margins from the five-year minimum, median and maximum, and working capital from history, and label each as such.

3. Working capital is not optional
Growth consumes cash: receivables and inventory rise with sales. A model that ignores the change in working capital overstates free cash flow for any growing business, and the faster the growth the larger the error. Negative working capital businesses, such as some retailers and software subscriptions, generate cash as they grow; the sign matters and the history says which case applies.

4. Reinvestment and growth must agree
Growth without reinvestment is a free lunch the model should not serve. Sustainable growth is roughly the reinvestment rate times the return on invested capital. A scenario that projects 15 percent growth with reinvestment consistent with 5 percent is internally inconsistent, and the sanity checks compare projected growth with the historical relationship.

5. Terminal value carries most of the answer
For most businesses the terminal value is more than half of the enterprise value, often three quarters. The share is reported alongside the value so the reader can see how much rests on the perpetuity assumption. The next note is about that assumption.
