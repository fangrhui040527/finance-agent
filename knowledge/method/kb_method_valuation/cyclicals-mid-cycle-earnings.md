1. The trap
At the top of a cycle a cyclical company reports record earnings and its trailing price-to-earnings looks low. At the bottom it reports losses and the multiple is undefined or absurd. Valuing on the trailing multiple buys at the peak and sells at the trough. The peak margin is the number the market extrapolates and the number that mean-reverts.

2. Mid-cycle earnings power
Normalise: take the average margin across a full cycle, apply it to current or trend revenue, and value that. For a commodity producer, take a mid-cycle price for the commodity and the company's cost position; a producer in the lowest cost quartile earns through the trough and is worth more per unit of capacity than one in the top quartile. The five-year history in the fact book is the minimum window; a full cycle can be longer.

3. Balance sheet through the trough
A cyclical with high leverage at the peak faces the trough with maturities it cannot refinance. Net debt to mid-cycle EBITDA, not peak EBITDA, is the leverage figure that matters. Sapura Energy in the failure library is the local case of peak-cycle expansion funded with debt.

4. What the multiple should look like
A cyclical near its peak trades on a low trailing multiple and a high mid-cycle multiple; near its trough the reverse. The valuation agent's archetype table sets the method to mid-cycle earnings power and marks the trailing P/E as not applicable. A memo on an aluminium smelter, a petrochemical producer or a memory-chip maker that quotes a trailing multiple has used the forbidden tool.

5. Signals of position in the cycle
Capacity additions industry-wide, inventory building at customers, spreads between the product and its feedstock, and the company's own capital expenditure relative to depreciation. Peak capital expenditure tends to arrive near peak prices, which is why it is a warning rather than a comfort.
