1. Why three scenarios and not a sensitivity table
A sensitivity table shows what happens if one input moves while the others stay put, which never happens. Scenarios move the inputs together in a way that could be true of the world: in the bear case growth slows and margins compress and the multiple falls; they are correlated. Three named scenarios with their assumptions written down are the minimum for a range that means something.

2. How the defaults are built here
The base case takes revenue growth from the five-year compound rate, capped at the terminal ceiling, the median historical margin, and historical reinvestment. The bear case halves the growth and uses the historical minimum margin. The bull case raises growth by half again, capped, and uses the historical maximum margin. Each default is labelled with the series it came from, and a person can override any of them by writing the reason. The output is the range between the bear and the bull, with the base marked.

3. What a scenario is not
It is not a probability-weighted target. Assigning 25, 50 and 25 percent to the three cases and averaging them produces a point that hides the width, which is the information. The range is reported as a range; a probability distribution across it needs more than three points and more evidence than a first workup has.

4. Bear cases fail for structural reasons
The bear case should be built from the failure patterns, not by shaving a few points off the base: what happens if receivables turn out to be uncollectible, if a covenant is breached, if the cycle turns. The failure library's analogues are the checklist for the bear case, and a bear case that is merely a slower base case has not been imagined hard enough.

5. Sanity applies to every scenario
The bull case is where the sanity checks earn their keep: terminal growth above the risk-free rate and margins above history appear first there. A bull case that fails a fatal check is dropped and the range narrows; the memo says so.
