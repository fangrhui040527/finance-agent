1. Where the value comes from
A regulated utility earns an allowed return on a regulated asset base for a regulatory period, with revenue set to recover operating costs, depreciation and that return. Value is therefore the asset base times the premium or discount the market applies to the allowed return, plus whatever unregulated businesses the group carries. Growth is capital expenditure that enters the base, not sales effort.

2. The questions that move the valuation
What is the allowed return and when is it reset. How is the cost of fuel or other pass-through handled, and with what lag. What incentive mechanisms reward or penalise performance against targets. How large is the capital plan relative to the existing base, and is it approved. What share of earnings is unregulated and how is that part valued.

3. Malaysia's frame
Tenaga's regulated business runs under incentive-based regulation set by the Energy Commission, with regulatory periods of several years and a fuel cost pass-through mechanism reviewed on a set cadence. The reset dates and the pass-through reviews are the scheduled events that matter; a policy change announced between them is the unscheduled one.

4. Valuation mechanics
Enterprise value to regulated asset base is the sector's multiple, and a DCF works because cash flows are contractual in character; the discount rate should reflect the low operating risk, and the terminal assumption should reflect that the base grows with approved capital expenditure, not with the economy. Dividend yield against the risk-free rate is the retail investor's shortcut and a fair first look.

5. Risks specific to the archetype
Regulatory reset below expectations; political intervention in tariffs; stranded assets in an energy transition; a capital plan financed with debt that regulators later disallow. None of these are visible in a trailing multiple.

6. References
The Energy Commission for the Malaysian framework; Ofgem's RIIO documents for a fully worked example of asset-base regulation.
