1. Why the usual model breaks
For a bank, debt is raw material rather than financing, and capital expenditure is regulatory capital rather than plant. Free cash flow to the firm is not a meaningful quantity when deposits are the product. Valuation therefore works on equity directly: what the bank earns on its book equity, against what shareholders require, decides whether the shares deserve more or less than book value.

2. The core relationship
A bank earning a return on equity above its cost of equity, sustainably, is worth more than book; one earning below is worth less. The justified price-to-book multiple is roughly (return on equity minus growth) divided by (cost of equity minus growth). A Malaysian bank with a 10 percent return on equity, a 9 percent cost of equity and 4 percent growth justifies about 1.2 times book; a bank on 1.6 times book is pricing in a higher return or lower risk than that.

3. The quality questions behind the return
Net interest margin and its sensitivity to policy rates; the loan book's mix and the provisioning charge through the cycle; non-interest income as a share of the total; the cost-to-income ratio; capital ratios against the regulatory minimum and the buffer above it; deposit franchise strength, measured by the share of current and savings accounts. A high return on equity built on thin capital or under-provisioning is a return that will be given back.

4. Duration and liquidity on a bank balance sheet
The 2023 failures showed that a bank's asset duration against its deposit stickiness is a valuation input, not a footnote. Unrealised losses on held-to-maturity securities are disclosed and can be netted against capital to see the economic position.
