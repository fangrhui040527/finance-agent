1. Two different economics under one heading
Subscription software has recurring revenue, gross margins in the seventies or eighties, and operating margins that depend on how much is spent acquiring the next customer. Semiconductors are a capital-intensive cyclical with technology transitions; the largest designers outsource manufacturing and look like software on margins, the manufacturers look like heavy industry. The archetype has to be chosen by the economics, not the sector label. Nvidia is a fabless designer riding a capital-expenditure cycle at its customers; TSMC is the manufacturer.

2. What to value when there is no margin
For a business that is not yet profitable, earnings multiples are undefined and revenue multiples ignore the cost of growth. Enterprise value to gross profit is the least bad multiple, because gross profit is what remains to fund everything else; paired with a stated path to an operating margin, it becomes a reverse DCF: what margin at what scale does this price require. The valuation agent marks trailing earnings multiples not applicable for software and any forward multiple not applicable for pre-profit names, and requires the reverse DCF instead.

3. Unit economics
Customer acquisition cost against lifetime value, net revenue retention, and the payback period on acquisition spend are the drivers behind the margin path. Net retention above 100 percent means the installed base grows without new customers; below it, growth depends entirely on acquisition. These are disclosed figures for listed software companies and belong in the workup even though they are not in the statements.

4. Semiconductor cycles and concentration
Semiconductor revenue follows customer capital-expenditure cycles with inventory corrections that can cut revenue by a third in a year. Customer concentration is a named risk: a designer with a handful of hyperscale customers has single-customer concentration in the failure library's vocabulary. Supply-chain dependence on one manufacturer or one region is the other structural exposure, and the knowledge graph's supply edges are where it is recorded.

5. The base-rate question
How many companies have sustained 25 percent revenue growth for a decade from a base of tens of billions. Few. The reverse DCF's implied growth for a large-cap growth name should be read against that base rate before any story about the technology is entertained.
