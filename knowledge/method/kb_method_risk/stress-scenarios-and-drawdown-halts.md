1. Stated shocks, not calm correlations
A stress test applies a named scenario to the book: the local index down 20 percent, the ringgit down 10 percent against the dollar, one sector down 40 percent, liquidity at a quarter of the twenty-day average. It reports the loss under each and the loss beyond the stops if gaps are assumed. Calm-period correlations are not used because they do not hold in the scenario being tested.

2. The drawdown ladder
Pre-committed actions at pre-committed drawdown levels. In this system the paper book halts new entries at an 8 percent drawdown from its peak while still allowing reductions, so the book can de-risk but not add. A real book would carry a further rung at which positions are cut to a fraction and a rung at which the process itself is reviewed. The rungs are written down in advance because the moment they are needed is the moment judgement is worst.

3. What the test cannot tell you
That the scenario will happen, or which one will. Its value is in the shape of the book it reveals: a book that loses 25 percent in the index-down scenario is more concentrated in market beta than its holder thought, whatever the name count.

4. Halts are not stops
A stop closes a position because its thesis failed. A halt stops new risk because the book's drawdown says the process is not working in the current regime. Confusing the two leads to holding a failed thesis because the book is fine, or cutting a sound one because the book is not.

5. References
The Basel principles for what a sound stress test contains; Bank Negara's Financial Stability Review for the scenarios applied to the Malaysian banking system, which are a reasonable starting set for a Malaysian equity book.
