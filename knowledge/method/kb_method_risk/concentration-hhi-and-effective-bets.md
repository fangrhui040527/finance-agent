1. Two ways to count
The Herfindahl index is the sum of squared portfolio weights; its reciprocal is the number of equal-weight positions that would give the same concentration. A book of five equal positions has an index of 0.20; one of ten has 0.10. The effective number of bets goes further and counts independent sources of return from the covariance matrix, so that five correlated banks count as roughly one.

2. The limits in this system
The Herfindahl limit is 0.18. The minimum effective number of bets is five, with a hard floor of three below which no entry is allowed. A single name is capped at 8 percent of the book with a hard ceiling of 15. A sector at 25 percent, a country at 40, a correlation cluster at 30. Each limit is checked on every sizing and rebalance, and the binding one is named.

3. Why both measures
The Herfindahl index catches a book that is concentrated by weight; the effective number of bets catches one that is concentrated by correlation while looking diversified by count. A Malaysian book of six Bursa names across banking, utilities, chemicals, healthcare, metals and gaming has more effective bets than six banks would, and fewer than the count suggests when the ringgit and the local index move them together.

4. Concentration is a choice, not an accident
A concentrated book is legitimate when the concentration is chosen, sized for the drawdown it can produce, and named in the journal. The limits exist so that concentration is never the accidental result of adding positions one at a time.
