1. The definition
The value of a business to its owners is the present value of the cash it will return to them, discounted at a rate that reflects the time value of money and the risk of the cash not arriving. Everything else in valuation is a way of estimating those two things: the cash flows and the discount rate. A discounted cash flow model makes the estimate explicit; a multiple hides it inside one number.

2. Why multiples are shorthand
A price-to-earnings ratio of 20 is a compressed statement about growth, return on capital and risk. Two businesses on the same multiple can have opposite prospects: one growing fast at low returns on capital, one growing slowly at high returns. Multiples are useful because they are fast and comparable; they are dangerous because they let the user skip the assumptions. Every multiple can be rewritten as a DCF with the assumptions made visible, and doing so once per name is the discipline.

3. Three contexts a multiple needs
A multiple means nothing on its own. It needs the name's own history (is 20 high or low for this business), its peers (is 20 high or low for this kind of business today), and a growth adjustment (is 20 justified by expected growth at this return on capital). This system's valuation agent reports the own-history percentile and, when a peer set and the figures exist, the peer band; a multiple reported without those three contexts is not evidence.

4. The method depends on the business
Banks are valued on book value against return on equity because their assets are financial and their earnings are leverage-dependent. Utilities on regulated asset base and allowed returns. Cyclicals on mid-cycle earnings, never on peak. Software on gross profit or revenue with a path to margin stated. Holding companies on the sum of their parts with a discount. Applying a trailing P/E to a cyclical at the top of its cycle is the classic error, and the valuation agent's archetype table exists to refuse it.

5. In this system
The valuation agent reports ranges and implied assumptions, never a point target. The cost-of-capital table, the scenario DCF and the peer comps in the valuation engines make the assumptions explicit, and the sanity checks refuse scenarios whose assumptions contradict the risk-free rate or the business's own history.
