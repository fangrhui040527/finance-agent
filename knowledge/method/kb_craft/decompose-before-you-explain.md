1. The most common analytical error
Naming a company-specific reason for a market-wide move. On a day the index fell 2 percent and the stock fell 2.5 percent, the company explains at most half a point, and the news item that ran that morning may explain none of it. The order of operations is fixed: subtract the market, subtract the sector, look at what is left, and only then ask what could have caused the residual. Cutler, Poterba and Summers found that most large market moves have no identifiable news at all; the same is true of single names more often than the financial press suggests.

2. Base rates first
Before asking what this earnings miss means for this company, ask what an earnings miss of this size has meant for companies of this size in this market historically: the distribution of next-day and next-month moves. That is the outside view, and Kahneman's finding is that people skip it and reason from the specific story, which is why their forecasts are overconfident. The event base-rate table in this system exists to make the outside view the default; a thesis that cites a catalyst without its base rate has skipped the step.

3. What makes a cause credible
A credible cause is specific to the name rather than the sector, arrived before the move rather than after it, is proportionate to the size of the move, and agrees with the direction. A sector-wide headline is a weak candidate for a single-name residual. A story published after the move is a description, not a cause. A rumour of a small contract does not explain a 10 percent fall. The catalyst-scoring engine ranks candidates on exactly these dimensions.

4. The honest answer
Sometimes the residual has no identified catalyst. Saying so is a finding, not a failure; the alternative is manufacturing a story that later gets treated as evidence. The feedback pages in this system record "unexplained" as a category with its share, and a large unexplained share on a large move is itself a reason for caution about the name.
