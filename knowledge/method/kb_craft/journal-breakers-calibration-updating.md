1. Write the thesis before the entry
A thesis written after a position exists is a rationalisation. The journal entry names the instrument, the stance, the horizon, the one-sentence reason, what must be true for it to work, and the evidence gaps that remain. Written first, it can be graded later; written later, it will be edited to fit. The prediction log in this system is append-only for this reason: entries can be added and graded, never changed.

2. Breakers: deciding now how you will be proven wrong
A breaker is a pre-committed condition under which the thesis is abandoned, with a date on which it is reviewed. "Revenue growth below 10 percent for two consecutive quarters, reviewed at each results date" is a breaker. "If things get worse" is not. Breakers convert a future emotional decision into a present analytical one, when the emotions are not yet engaged. A thesis with fewer than two breakers is not actionable in this system, and a breaker without a review date is flagged.

3. The pre-mortem
Before committing, assume it is two years later and the position has lost half its value, and write the most likely reasons. Klein's finding is that this framing surfaces risks that a forward-looking "what could go wrong" question does not, because it licenses people to describe failure concretely. The failure library in this system supplies analogues by structural pattern for this exercise: what resembled this situation, and how it ended.

4. Calibration: are your 70 percents actually 70 percent
Calibration is measured, not felt. When a forecaster's 70 percent calls come true about 70 percent of the time, they are calibrated; the classic finding, from Alpert and Raiffa onward, is that untrained people's 80 percent confidence intervals contain the truth well under 60 percent of the time. The Brier score summarises accuracy and calibration together. Neither can be computed from fewer than a few dozen graded forecasts, which is why the calibration table in this system refuses to report below its minimum count rather than showing a flattering small sample.

5. Changing your mind cheaply
Superforecasters update often and in small steps, treating each new fact as evidence rather than as a threat to a position. The cost of changing a view should be near zero; the cost of not changing it, when a breaker fires, is the whole position. The reflection agent here proposes lessons only from graded outcomes, with a bias toward "no lesson" when the evidence is thin, because a lesson learned from noise is a new error.
