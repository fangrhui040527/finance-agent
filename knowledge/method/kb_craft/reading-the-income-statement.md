1. What the statement claims
The income statement says: in this period the business earned this revenue, incurred these costs, and kept this profit. Every line below revenue is a subtraction, and every subtraction involves a judgement about which period a cost belongs to. Depreciation spreads a purchase over an estimated life. Provisions recognise a loss before the cash leaves. Capitalised development turns an expense into an asset. Each is legitimate, and each can be stretched.

2. Profit is an opinion about timing
The single misconception this note exists to kill is that profit is cash. It is not. Profit is revenue recognised minus expenses matched, and recognition and matching are choices bounded by standards, not facts of nature. A company can report record profit while its cash from operations falls for three years running; that pattern is the first thing the earnings-quality checks in this system look for.

3. Where to look first
Gross margin, because it is closest to the physical business and hardest to manage with accounting choices. Operating margin, because it shows whether the overhead scales. The gap between operating profit and net profit, because interest, tax and one-off items live there and one-offs that recur every year are not one-offs. Revenue growth against receivables growth, because receivables outrunning sales means revenue is being booked faster than customers are paying.

4. Questions the statement cannot answer alone
Whether the profit turned into cash (the cash flow statement). Whether the assets that produced it are real and appropriately valued (the balance sheet). Whether the accounting policies changed between years (the notes). An income statement read without the other two is a press release with a table.

5. In this system
The fact book stores revenue, gross profit, operating income, net income and diluted EPS per period with the date each became knowable, and the ratio layer computes margins and their trend from those rows rather than trusting a vendor's summary.
