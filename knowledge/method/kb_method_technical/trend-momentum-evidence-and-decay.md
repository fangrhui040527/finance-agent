1. What has been documented
Cross-sectional momentum, buying the past year's winners and selling its losers, earned positive returns in most markets and decades studied, with a tendency to crash in sharp reversals. Time-series momentum, going with each asset's own past return, shows similar evidence across asset classes. These are statistical findings about portfolios of hundreds of names; they say little about any single stock on any single day.

2. Decay after publication
McLean and Pontiff found that published anomalies lose roughly a third to a half of their return after publication as capital moves in. A pattern that worked in a paper from 1993 is not evidence that it works in Bursa mid-caps in 2026; it is a hypothesis to test on this system's own history.

3. Mean reversion at other horizons
Returns show reversal at very short horizons, a week or a month, and at very long ones, three to five years, with momentum in between. A single "trend" label without a horizon is meaningless, and the horizon has to match the holding period of the thesis.

4. What the technical agent does with this
It describes: the direction and size of the move over stated windows, against the stock's own volatility. It does not predict from the description. A pattern becomes citable evidence only once a base rate has been measured on this market, this cap band and this horizon, with a sample large enough to be marked validated.

5. References
Jegadeesh and Titman for cross-sectional momentum; Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen for time-series momentum; McLean and Pontiff for post-publication decay.
