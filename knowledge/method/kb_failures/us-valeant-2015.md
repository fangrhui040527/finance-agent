1. What the numbers showed beforehand
A pharmaceutical company that grew by acquiring others with borrowed money, cutting research spending, and raising the prices of acquired drugs. Reported "cash earnings" excluded acquisition costs and amortisation that recurred every year. Organic growth, once acquisitions were stripped out, was much lower than the headline. Debt reached roughly thirty billion dollars.

2. What happened
In 2015 a specialty pharmacy through which the company sold a large share of certain products was revealed to be an undisclosed, effectively controlled channel. Revenue recognition was restated, the share price fell about 90 percent from its peak, and the debt that had funded the acquisitions became a covenant problem in a business whose growth story had ended.

3. Pattern
A serial acquirer whose adjusted earnings hid the recurring cost of the strategy; related-party dependence through a distribution channel; accruals diverging from cash; and a covenant cliff when the multiple that supported the borrowing collapsed.

4. The check in this system that would have caught it
Organic growth separated from acquired growth; the gap between adjusted and reported earnings tracked over years; net debt to EBITDA on reported figures; return on invested capital including the acquisition premiums paid. The red team's survivorship and mechanism challenges apply: the thesis rested on a strategy whose failures had been removed from the comparison set.

The Senate committee report is public domain; the company's own filings carry the restatement.
