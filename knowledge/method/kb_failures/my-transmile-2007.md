1. What the numbers showed beforehand
An air cargo operator whose reported revenue and profit grew quickly through 2005 and 2006 while trade receivables grew faster still, and while cash generated from operations did not keep pace with the profit being reported. Growth was booked, not collected. A large shareholder with a strong reputation lent the story credibility that the working-capital figures did not support.

2. What happened
In 2007 the auditor declined to sign off on the accounts and a special audit found that revenue for 2005 and 2006 had been substantially overstated through fictitious sales. The results were restated, the share price collapsed, directors and officers were charged by the Securities Commission, and the company was eventually delisted.

3. Pattern
Receivables outrunning revenue for more than a year; reported profit diverging from operating cash flow; an audit dispute as the first public sign; and, underneath, sales that did not exist.

4. The check in this system that would have caught it
Receivable days trending up against flat or rising revenue in the ratio sheet; the accrual ratio and the Beneish days-sales-in-receivables index in the quality engine; the auditor-change and going-concern event kinds in the fact book once a statement source covers Bursa names. Today, for a Bursa name, the check reports that it lacks the statement lines and names the source that would supply them.
