1. What the numbers showed beforehand
A property developer funding construction with presale deposits from buyers, commercial paper to suppliers, wealth-management products sold to staff and retail investors, and bank and bond borrowings, all on top of reported debt that already breached the leverage limits regulators introduced in 2020. Contract liabilities, money owed to buyers for homes not yet built, were a funding source that did not appear as debt.

2. What happened
When the regulator's limits cut off new borrowing, the treadmill stopped. Suppliers went unpaid, presales slowed, and in December 2021 the group defaulted on offshore bonds. Restructuring failed and a Hong Kong court ordered its liquidation in 2024.

3. Pattern
A covenant cliff imposed by regulation rather than lenders; leverage hidden in liabilities that were not called debt; and a capital-raise treadmill in which each source of funding depended on the growth the others financed.

4. The check in this system that would have caught it
Total liabilities, not just borrowings, against assets; the share of funding from customers and suppliers; interest cover including off-balance-sheet obligations. The general lesson is that a business whose model requires ever-larger inflows to service the last round's obligations is a treadmill regardless of its sector.
