1. What the numbers showed beforehand
Reported earnings grew steadily while cash from operations was erratic and, in several periods, far below net income; mark-to-market accounting recognised the present value of long-dated contracts as current profit. Debt was moved into special-purpose entities managed by the company's own chief financial officer, disclosed in footnotes as related-party transactions that few read. Return on capital was low for a business reporting such growth.

2. What happened
In 2001 the company disclosed a large equity write-down connected to the related-party entities, restated earnings back to 1997, lost the market's confidence in weeks, and filed for bankruptcy in December. Its auditor was convicted of obstruction and ceased to exist.

3. Pattern
Accruals diverging from cash for years; leverage hidden in entities the statements did not consolidate; related-party dependence disclosed but not understood; and an auditor whose independence had been compromised by consulting fees.

4. The check in this system that would have caught it
The accrual ratio over several years; net income against cash from operations; the related-party disclosures as an event kind; return on invested capital against the cost of capital, which was negative for much of the period the market paid a growth multiple. The red team's standing challenge on accounting is this case in one sentence.

5. References
The Senate subcommittee report is public domain and is the primary secondary source; the Powers Report is the board's own account.
